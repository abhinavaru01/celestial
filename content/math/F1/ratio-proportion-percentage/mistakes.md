# Common Mistakes — Ratio, Proportion & Percentage

### 1. Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole
"Boys to girls is 2 : 3, so boys are 2/3 of the class." **Wrong** — that's part-to-part. Boys are $\frac{2}{2+3} = \frac{2}{5}$ of the class.
> **Fix:** the instant you see a ratio, ask "am I comparing two parts, or a part to the whole?" Add the parts to get the whole *before* forming any fraction of the total.

### 2. Comparing ratios without the same units
"3 kg : 500 g $= 3 : 500$." **Wrong.** Convert first: $3000\text{ g} : 500\text{ g} = 6 : 1$.
> **Fix:** same unit on both sides before you do anything else. A ratio has no units, but the quantities must share one.

### 3. Percentage change divided by the wrong number
Price goes 20 → 25. "Change is 5, and $5/25 = 20\%$." **Wrong** — you divided by the new price.
> **Fix:** % change is always $\frac{\text{change}}{\textbf{original}}\times100 = \frac{5}{20}\times100 = 25\%$. Underline the original value before you divide.

### 4. Reverse-percentage: undoing a % by applying the same %
"Price after 20% off is ₹480, so original $= 480 + 20\%$ of $480 = 576$." **Wrong.** The 20% was taken off the *original*, not the sale price.
> **Fix:** write the relationship as an equation. Sale price is 80% of original: $0.8x = 480 \Rightarrow x = 600$. Note $600 \ne 576$.

### 5. Adding successive percentages
"Up 10% then down 10% → back to start." **Wrong** — you end at 99% of the start.
> **Fix:** multiply factors: $1.10 \times 0.90 = 0.99$. Percentages of *different* bases never simply add.

### 6. Mixing up direct and inverse proportion
"More workers, so it takes more days" or blindly cross-multiplying an inverse relationship.
> **Fix:** ask "if this quantity goes **up**, does the other go up (direct, $y=kx$) or down (inverse, $xy=k$)?" More workers → fewer days → inverse → keep the **product** constant.

### 7. Treating "of" and "off" as the same
"20% of 480" vs "20% off 480" — students compute one when the problem means the other.
> **Fix:** *of* = multiply ($0.20\times480 = 96$). *Off* = subtract that from the total ($480 - 96 = 384$).

### 8. Forgetting a percentage is dimensionless (and the reference matters)
"50% is bigger than 40%, so a 50% pay rise is more money than a 40% rise." Not necessarily — 50% of a small salary can be less than 40% of a large one.
> **Fix:** a percentage always refers to *something*. Two percentages are only comparable if they're percentages **of the same base**.

### 9. Simplifying a ratio by subtracting instead of dividing
"$6 : 4$, subtract 2 from each → $4 : 2$." Coincidentally looks reducible but the method is wrong and usually fails ($6:4 - 3 = 3:1 \ne 3:2$).
> **Fix:** simplify ratios by **dividing** both parts by a common factor, never subtracting.

### 10. Losing the "one part" step when dividing in a ratio
Splitting ₹600 in $2:3$ and writing A $= ₹2$, B $= ₹3$.
> **Fix:** find the value of **one part** first ($600 \div 5 = 120$), then multiply. Parts are shares of the whole, not rupee amounts themselves.
