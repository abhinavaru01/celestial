# Common Mistakes — Motion

### 1. Averaging two speeds directly
"30 km/h for half the trip and 60 km/h for the other half → average 45 km/h." **Wrong** when the halves are equal *distances*.
> **Fix:** average speed = total distance ÷ total time, *always*. For equal distances at 30 and 60, it's 40 km/h. Compute the two times and add them. (This is the same "average of rates" trap from the ratio topic.)

### 2. Confusing distance with displacement (and speed with velocity)
Reporting velocity as non-zero for a round trip that returns to the start.
> **Fix:** displacement is *net* change in position with direction; a round trip has zero displacement, hence zero average velocity, even though distance and average speed are not zero.

### 3. Thinking constant speed means zero acceleration — even around a curve
"The car's speedometer reads a steady 40, so acceleration is zero."
> **Fix:** velocity includes direction. Turning changes direction, so velocity changes, so there *is* acceleration even at constant speed. Zero acceleration requires constant speed **and** constant direction.

### 4. Mixing up the slope of a d–t graph with a v–t graph
Reading "area" off a distance–time graph, or "slope" as distance.
> **Fix:** on a **distance–time** graph, slope = speed (area means nothing). On a **velocity–time** graph, slope = acceleration and **area = distance**. Label which graph you're looking at before extracting anything.

### 5. Using the uniform-acceleration equations when acceleration isn't constant
Applying $v = u + at$ to a car whose acceleration is changing.
> **Fix:** $v=u+at$, $s=ut+\tfrac12at^2$, $v^2=u^2+2as$ hold **only** for constant acceleration. If a graph is curved (a not constant), you can't use them over that stretch.

### 6. Unit inconsistency (mixing km/h and m/s in one equation)
Plugging a speed in km/h and a time in seconds into the same equation.
> **Fix:** convert everything to a single system first — usually SI (metres, seconds, m/s, m/s²). Remember ÷3.6 for km/h → m/s.

### 7. Sign errors with deceleration
Treating a slowing object's acceleration as positive, or forgetting the minus sign.
> **Fix:** choose a positive direction and stick to it. If the object slows, $a$ has the opposite sign to the velocity. In free fall taking "up" as positive, $a = -g$.

### 8. Forgetting the ½ in s = ut + ½at²
Writing $s = ut + at^2$.
> **Fix:** the ½ comes from the *triangle* area under the v–t graph. Picture the triangle and the ½ is unforgettable.

### 9. Assuming an object at rest has no acceleration
"It's momentarily stopped at the top of its throw, so a = 0 there."
> **Fix:** at the top of a vertical throw, velocity is zero *for an instant* but acceleration is still $g$ downward — that's exactly why it starts falling. Velocity = 0 ≠ acceleration = 0.

### 10. Reading the area under a v–t graph as speed instead of distance
> **Fix:** area = (velocity) × (time) = distance/displacement. The *height* is velocity; the *area* is how far it went.
